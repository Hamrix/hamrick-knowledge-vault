# Early records naming Major Selby cannot yet be merged into one man

## Claim status

The adult 1783 Worcester taxpayer, the underage 1785 orphan carried in guardian accounts through 1800, the 1808 Worcester marriage-index entry, the adult Bourbon cluster documented from 1807 through 1815, the 1820 Nicholas household and 1833 Nicholas testator, the separate 1820 Somerset household, and the later Indiana Major cannot all be merged by name alone.

## Analysis

The 1783 adult and 1785 child are necessarily distinct. The 1790 will and 1795 final account prove that the child was the son of deceased John Selby and grandson of John Selby Esq. Four consecutive guardian accounts carry that same child through February 1800. Ann Selby's estate account then calls Major her grandson and records payment directly to him by receipt and order in September 1803, carrying the same family identity into an adult transaction. A Bourbon Circuit Court packet directly places an adult Major Selby there in August 1807, and the uncommon name, county, adult status, and chronology strongly correlate that plaintiff with the 1811 Bourbon purchaser.

The central proposed migration gap is less than four years, from the Worcester estate payment on 30 September 1803 to the Bourbon suit begun on 5 August 1807. The 1807 case does not state birthplace, parents, or prior residence, and an unassigned adult Major Selby also executed a Kent County, Delaware, instrument in 1801. A derivative Worcester index then reports Major Selby and Hester Cottingham on 8 December 1808, after the Bourbon appearance; the original marriage record has not been found, so this may represent return travel, a different man, or a compilation error.

The 1811-1813 tax sequence independently corroborates and continues the Bourbon purchaser. The 1812 ejectment service and 1815 survey then place Major among the land occupants and label his house and improvement, extending the reviewed Bourbon cluster through August 1815. In 1817 Thomas Rule of Bourbon County, who is strongly correlated with the man who furnished Major's deed for the 1815 survey, acquired Nicholas County land in Haw's survey on Hinkston and Brushy Fork. A second tract was bounded by Valentine Cress, one of the 1812 occupants served with Major. Barkley Selby separately held 116 acres in the Samuel Haws survey on Brushy Fork from 1816, conveyed 18.5 acres with wife Patsey in 1817, and remained in possession of the 97.5-acre residue in October 1820. William Selby and wife Priscilla also conveyed a small Brushy Fork tract in 1819. This supplies a specific associate, surname-cluster, and land-geography bridge into Nicholas County records, but none of these deeds names Major or states a relationship among the Selby households.

Review of the Nicholas census images found one Major Selby household on page 111, while a different original census schedule records another Major Selby household in Somerset County, Maryland, in the same year. The Nicholas testator later named wife Peggy, son Henry, and daughter Polly Anterbus in his 1833 will. Because that will was proved in August 1833, he cannot be the Hamilton County, Indiana, Major alive in 1850 and 1860. The Kentucky and Indiana identity clusters are therefore conclusively separate.

The later Henry evidence adds a second chronological warning. An 1809 Bourbon bond names Henry Selby and Nelly Henderson as the intended couple. The 1821 Jesse Henderson division assigns Henry two interests, and deeds from 1823 through 1830 show him acquiring four more heir shares. An 1838 Nicholas deed names Henry's wife as Eleanor, and the 1850 Nicholas census records Henry, age 66 and born in Maryland, with Eleanor, age 55 and born in Maryland. The same paired names, county, Henderson network, and chronology strongly continue one local identity, although no record explicitly equates Nelly and Eleanor.

Margaret Selby's 1836 will now supplies the strongest family bridge. She calls Major her deceased husband and names Kitty and Duland as her granddaughters and daughters of Henry Selby; the probate order records Henry's opposition to establishment of the will. Independent marriage records call Catharine and Dulcina daughters of the Nicholas County Henry. These records strongly identify the later Henry as Major's son, even though no post-1833 instrument literally repeats that father-son wording and Margaret does not call Henry her son.

The later Henry's estimated 1784 birth makes a father who was only about seven in 1785 chronologically impossible. It also means the 1820 Nicholas household's male age 26-44 cannot automatically be treated as Henry's father or as the 1833 testator. The Worcester ward-to-Kentucky testator hypothesis is therefore materially conflicted and must not be promoted from the uncommon name and migration timing alone.

## Confidence rationale

`confirmed`: the 1783 adult-versus-1785 child collision and the 1833 Kentucky death-versus-1850 Indiana life collision decisively prove that multiple men are present. Margaret's will makes the Henry-based chronological conflict substantially stronger against merging the Worcester ward with the Nicholas testator. It does not identify the correct older Kentucky Major or prove that every Kentucky appearance belongs to one man.
